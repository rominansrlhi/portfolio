Case study · Digikala Mehr

Why the Questionnaire Stage Took More Than 30 Days

Self-initiated observation · Team: Digikala's Social Responsibility team · Focus: charity partner onboarding

Note: at the time, I didn't know this was called "UX research." I was working as a CSR Specialist, not a researcher — I just noticed something in the onboarding process wasn't working, and went looking for why.

The problem

Onboarding a charity partner onto Digikala Mehr moved through several steps — an initial phone call, an online interview, sending the enrollment questionnaire, completing it, finalizing the contract, sending it for signature, and activating the account. One step kept standing out: for a number of partners, completing the questionnaire was taking more than 30 days — far longer than filling out a form should reasonably take. My first assumption was that the questionnaire was simply "too long." This is the assumption I set out to test.

The onboarding flow

Phone call

Online interview

Questionnaire sent

Questionnaire completed — 30+ days

Contract finalized

Contract sent for signature

Account activated

Tracking these stages in ClickUp is what first made the questionnaire stage visible as the bottleneck.

Research questions

01

Where in the questionnaire stage do charities actually get stuck?

Locating the point of friction, rather than assuming it.

02

What technical, informational, or motivational barriers are involved?

Separating UX problems from process or policy problems.

03

What expectations do charities have that the process isn't meeting?

Surfacing the gap between what's expected and what's delivered.

How I looked into it

Informal follow-up conversations

Whenever a partner's onboarding stalled at the questionnaire stage, I reached out directly — by phone, an online call, or message — and asked what was making it hard to finish. There was no fixed script or target sample size; I simply followed up with whichever partners were stuck, for as long as this kept happening.

Stage tracking in ClickUp

I used ClickUp to see which stage of onboarding partners were sitting at, which is how the questionnaire stage stood out from the rest of the flow.

This wasn't run as a structured study — I didn't have a research background or a formal protocol at the time. The questions below are the kind I found myself asking in those conversations.

  • What made it difficult to complete the questionnaire?
  • Were there any questions you couldn't answer immediately? Why?
  • Did you need to collect information or documents from other people?
  • Was there anything that made you postpone or stop completing it?
  • What would have made this easier for you?

Findings

01

High information dependency created friction

Finding

Several of the partners I talked to described needing to pause and coordinate with a manager or colleague to track down information the questionnaire asked for — it wasn't something one person could always answer alone.

Insight

The flow implicitly assumed one person could access everything it asked for. In reality, this was often a multi-stakeholder task — progress depended on internal coordination the process never accounted for.

UX implication

Minimize information dependencies, and make clear up front exactly what will be needed before someone starts.

02

Questionnaire length increased the effort of finishing

Finding

Partners repeatedly described the questionnaire as long, and some questions felt unnecessary to answer before they could even begin engaging with Digikala Mehr. When the team later made a portion of the questions optional, the stage seemed to move faster for new partners — though I wasn't tracking this formally at the time, so I can't put an exact number on it.

Insight

Not every question carried equal weight for onboarding. Treating everything as mandatory inflated the perceived effort and gave people a reason to postpone finishing.

UX implication

Prioritize what's essential, and make the line between required and optional visible, not just technically true.

03

Documentation requirements added unnecessary burden

Finding

Partners also struggled to gather the documents the flow requested. Going through the list together with the Legal team, some of it turned out not to be strictly necessary.

Insight

Onboarding was asking for effort — information and paperwork — that wasn't always necessary to move the process forward.

UX implication

Every requested document should have a clear purpose, and should only be asked for at the stage it's actually needed.

04
Unresolved opportunity

No Save & Resume amplified the cost of interruption

Finding

When partners had to step away to gather missing information or documents, they couldn't save their progress — their answers were lost, and they had to restart the questionnaire.

Insight

The real problem wasn't only how much was being asked — it was the cost of stepping away. A task that already required coordinating with other people became a high-risk, all-or-nothing activity.

UX implication

A Save & Resume mechanism could lower the cost of interruption. This was identified during this process but never implemented — it's presented here as a recommendation for future work, not something already shipped.

The strongest overall insight

Onboarding was designed as a single-session task, but charities experienced it as a multi-stakeholder process requiring time, coordination, and access to information and documents.

Ethnographic study

When AI Becomes the First Step

An ethnography of students' everyday relationships with AI

Research question

How has AI moved, in students' everyday lives, from a helper tool into something closer to an assistant, an advisor, and at times a partner in decision-making? And where does a gap open up between how students talk about using AI and how they actually use it?

Method

Approach

Ethnographic study

Field

~20 university students

Data

Observation + informal, semi-structured interviews

What I found

AI showed up in students' lives in three overlapping roles: as an assistant for writing, translation, and coursework; as an advisor for personal decisions and relationships; and, for some, as something closer to a companion for daily conversation. The same person could move between all three roles in a single day.

A few patterns stood out. Students often described using AI as "just getting help," but in practice, some had reached a point where starting a task without it felt difficult — the line between help and dependency wasn't always visible from the inside. When instructors caught AI-generated work, the reaction usually wasn't "I shouldn't have used it," but "I should have used it in a way that couldn't be noticed" — a new kind of AI literacy built around not getting caught rather than around whether to use it at all. And when it came to personal or moral decisions, students didn't always turn to AI for a different perspective; often they described their own version of events and used its response to validate a decision they'd already leaned toward.

The broader takeaway: the interesting question isn't whether students use AI, but the point at which it stops being an external tool and becomes part of how someone thinks, decides, and justifies their own choices to themselves.
